Here is an improved solution. The gap identified in the bug report is fixed by proving the “lowest terms” step as a separate lemma.

\[
\text{For integers } m,n,\text{ write } m\mid n \text{ to mean that } n=mk \text{ for some } k\in\mathbb Z.
\]

**Lemma 1.** If \(n\in\mathbb Z\) and \(7\mid n^2\), then \(7\mid n\).

**Proof.** By the division algorithm, since \(7>0\), there exist integers \(a\) and \(r\) such that

\[
n=7a+r
\]

with

\[
r\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5,6\}.
\]

Then

\[
n^2=(7a+r)^2=49a^2+14ar+r^2.
\]

Hence

\[
n^2-r^2=49a^2+14ar=7(7a^2+2ar),
\]

so \(7\mid(n^2-r^2)\). Therefore, if \(7\mid n^2\), then \(7\mid r^2\).

Now check the possible values of \(r\):

\[
\begin{array}{c|ccccccc}
r & 0 & 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\
\hline
r^2 \bmod 7 & 0 & 1 & 4 & 2 & 2 & 4 & 1
\end{array}
\]

The only case in which \(7\mid r^2\) is \(r=0\). Thus \(r=0\), so

\[
n=7a.
\]

Therefore \(7\mid n\). \(\square\)

**Lemma 2.** If \(p,q\in\mathbb Z\) and \(q\neq 0\), then there exist integers \(a,b\) such that

\[
\frac{p}{q}=\frac{a}{b}, \qquad b\neq 0,
\]

and \(a\) and \(b\) have no positive common divisor greater than \(1\).

**Proof.** Let

\[
D=\{d\in\mathbb Z_{>0}: d\mid p \text{ and } d\mid q\}.
\]

The set \(D\) is nonempty because \(1\in D\). Also, if \(d\in D\), then \(d\mid q\), so \(q=dt\) for some nonzero integer \(t\). Hence

\[
|q|=d|t|\ge d,
\]

so \(d\le |q|\). Therefore

\[
D\subseteq \{1,2,\dots,|q|\},
\]

which is a finite set. Thus \(D\) has a largest element; call it \(d\).

Since \(d\mid p\) and \(d\mid q\), there exist integers \(a,b\) such that

\[
p=da,\qquad q=db.
\]

Because \(q\neq 0\) and \(d>0\), we have \(b\neq 0\). Also,

\[
\frac{p}{q}=\frac{da}{db}=\frac{a}{b}.
\]

We claim that \(a\) and \(b\) have no positive common divisor greater than \(1\). Suppose, for contradiction, that some integer \(c>1\) divides both \(a\) and \(b\). Then \(a=ca_1\) and \(b=cb_1\) for some integers \(a_1,b_1\). Hence

\[
p=da=dc a_1,
\qquad
q=db=dc b_1.
\]

Thus \(dc\mid p\) and \(dc\mid q\), so \(dc\in D\). But \(c>1\) and \(d>0\), so

\[
dc>d,
\]

contradicting the choice of \(d\) as the largest element of \(D\). Therefore \(a\) and \(b\) have no positive common divisor greater than \(1\). \(\square\)

**Theorem.** The real number \(\sqrt{7}\) is irrational.

**Proof.** Suppose, for contradiction, that \(\sqrt{7}\) is not irrational. By the given definition, this means that there exist integers \(p,q\) with \(q\neq 0\) such that

\[
\sqrt{7}=\frac{p}{q}.
\]

By Lemma 2, we may choose integers \(a,b\) such that

\[
\sqrt{7}=\frac{a}{b}, \qquad b\neq 0,
\]

and \(a\) and \(b\) have no positive common divisor greater than \(1\).

Squaring both sides gives

\[
7=\frac{a^2}{b^2}.
\]

Since \(b\neq 0\), we may multiply by \(b^2\), obtaining

\[
7b^2=a^2.
\]

Thus \(7\mid a^2\). By Lemma 1, it follows that

\[
7\mid a.
\]

Therefore there exists an integer \(k\) such that

\[
a=7k.
\]

Substituting this into \(7b^2=a^2\), we get

\[
7b^2=(7k)^2=49k^2.
\]

Dividing both sides by \(7\), we obtain

\[
b^2=7k^2.
\]

Hence \(7\mid b^2\). By Lemma 1 again,

\[
7\mid b.
\]

So \(7\mid a\) and \(7\mid b\). Therefore the positive integer \(7>1\) is a common divisor of \(a\) and \(b\), contradicting the fact that \(a\) and \(b\) have no positive common divisor greater than \(1\).

This contradiction shows that no integers \(p,q\) with \(q\neq 0\) satisfy

\[
\sqrt{7}=\frac{p}{q}.
\]

Therefore, by the given definition, \(\sqrt{7}\) is irrational. \(\square\)
