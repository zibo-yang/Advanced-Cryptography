```latex
\begin{theorem}
The number $\sqrt{7}$ is irrational.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We first justify the use of a fraction in lowest terms. If $r\in\mathbb{Q}$, then by definition there exist integers $a,b$ with $b\neq 0$ such that
\[
r=\frac{a}{b}.
\]
Let
\[
d=\gcd(a,b).
\]
Then $d>0$, and since $d\mid a$ and $d\mid b$, there exist integers $p,q$ such that
\[
a=dp,\qquad b=dq.
\]
Since $b\neq 0$, we also have $q\neq 0$. Thus
\[
r=\frac{a}{b}=\frac{dp}{dq}=\frac{p}{q}.
\]
Moreover, $\gcd(p,q)=1$: indeed, if a positive integer $c$ divides both $p$ and $q$, then $dc$ divides both $a$ and $b$. Since $d=\gcd(a,b)$ is the greatest positive common divisor of $a$ and $b$, we must have $dc\le d$, hence $c\le 1$, so $c=1$.

Now suppose, for contradiction, that $\sqrt{7}$ is rational. By the preceding argument, there exist integers $p,q$ such that
\[
q\neq 0,\qquad \gcd(p,q)=1,\qquad \sqrt{7}=\frac{p}{q}.
\]
Squaring both sides gives
\[
7=\frac{p^2}{q^2}.
\]
Since $q\neq 0$, multiplying both sides by $q^2$ yields
\[
p^2=7q^2.
\]
Therefore
\[
7\mid p^2.
\]

We claim that for every integer $n$, if $7\mid n^2$, then $7\mid n$. To prove this, note that every integer is congruent modulo $7$ to exactly one of
\[
0,1,2,3,4,5,6.
\]
The corresponding squares modulo $7$ are
\[
0^2\equiv 0,\quad
1^2\equiv 1,\quad
2^2\equiv 4,\quad
3^2\equiv 2,\quad
4^2\equiv 2,\quad
5^2\equiv 4,\quad
6^2\equiv 1
\pmod{7}.
\]
Thus the only residue class whose square is congruent to $0$ modulo $7$ is the residue class $0$. Hence, if
\[
n^2\equiv 0\pmod{7},
\]
then
\[
n\equiv 0\pmod{7},
\]
which means exactly that $7\mid n$.

Applying this claim to $p$, we get
\[
7\mid p.
\]
So there exists an integer $k$ such that
\[
p=7k.
\]
Substituting this into $p^2=7q^2$ gives
\[
(7k)^2=7q^2,
\]
so
\[
49k^2=7q^2.
\]
Dividing both sides by $7$ gives
\[
7k^2=q^2.
\]
Therefore
\[
7\mid q^2.
\]
Applying the same claim to $q$, we obtain
\[
7\mid q.
\]

Thus $7$ divides both $p$ and $q$, so $p$ and $q$ have a common positive divisor greater than $1$. This contradicts
\[
\gcd(p,q)=1.
\]
Therefore our assumption that $\sqrt{7}$ is rational must be false. Hence $\sqrt{7}$ is irrational.
\end{proof}
```
