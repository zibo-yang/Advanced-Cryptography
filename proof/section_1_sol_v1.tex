\section*{Proof}

We prove that $\sqrt{7}$ is irrational.

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that $\sqrt{7}$ is rational. Then there exist integers $p$ and $q$ with $q \neq 0$ such that
\[
\sqrt{7}=\frac{p}{q},
\]
and we may choose such a representation with $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

Squaring both sides gives
\[
7=\frac{p^2}{q^2}.
\]
Multiplying by $q^2$, we obtain
\[
p^2=7q^2.
\]
Thus $7 \mid p^2$.

We claim that if $7 \mid p^2$, then $7 \mid p$. Indeed, since $7$ is prime, the only possible remainders of an integer modulo $7$ are
\[
0,1,2,3,4,5,6.
\]
Squaring these modulo $7$ gives
\[
0^2 \equiv 0,\quad
1^2 \equiv 1,\quad
2^2 \equiv 4,\quad
3^2 \equiv 2,\quad
4^2 \equiv 2,\quad
5^2 \equiv 4,\quad
6^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{7}.
\]
Therefore, the only way for $p^2 \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$ is for $p \equiv 0 \pmod{7}$. Hence $7 \mid p$.

So we may write
\[
p=7k
\]
for some integer $k$. Substituting this into $p^2=7q^2$ gives
\[
(7k)^2=7q^2.
\]
Thus
\[
49k^2=7q^2.
\]
Dividing both sides by $7$, we get
\[
7k^2=q^2.
\]
Therefore $7 \mid q^2$. By the same argument as before, this implies
\[
7 \mid q.
\]

We have shown that $7 \mid p$ and $7 \mid q$. Hence $p$ and $q$ have a common divisor greater than $1$, namely $7$. This contradicts the assumption that
\[
\gcd(p,q)=1.
\]

Therefore our supposition was false, and $\sqrt{7}$ is irrational.
